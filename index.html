How to read the diagram

Left to right is time, 1902 to 2026. Ridge height is the number of definitions of that kind printed around that year, smoothed over about a year either side. Every decade from the 1900s to the 2020s is represented.

The sorting into ten schools is an interpretation, made by reading each passage. A definition that fits two schools was placed with the idea it leans on hardest. The two ridges at the back, “A way of thinking” and “Many geographies,” were added on 27 November 2026 after a second reading found definitions that name a method or a plurality rather than a subject matter; the pins that moved there are ringed in white.

Quotations were cleaned of scanning errors. The file sometimes lists a passage under the article next to the one it came from, so check the DOI before citing.

What each definition is doing

On 27 November 2026 every defining passage in the sheet was read a second way, following Wittgenstein’s advice to ask not what a word means but what it is used for (Philosophical Investigations §§11, 43, 65–67, 421). Read like that, a definition of geography is an instrument deployed inside an institution. It does one of eight jobs: it defends turf against history, geology or the social studies; it admits or bars textbook content; it claims scientific or civic standing before an outside authority; it charters a subfield; it licenses a teaching method or a scale of testing; it repudiates an older definition to force reform; it refuses or mocks the question; or it hands on the canon. Each move has a colour. Switch the pins to “What the definition does” to see them on the terrain, and open any pin to read its move and the moment it was written in.

The grid counts all 772 coded passages, pinned or not, by move and decade; darker is more. The last row marks the shocks drawn on the terrain as amber walls: the founding of the AAG (1904) and the NCGT (1915), Harvard closing its geography department (1948), Sputnik (1957), the High School Geography Project (1961), A Nation at Risk (1983), the Five Themes (1984) and the National Geography Standards (1994). Select a cell to isolate that move and jump to its first pin in that decade.

Do the defensive moves cluster around the shocks? Within five years of one of those eight events sit 70% of the legitimation passages and 66% of the reform-signaling ones, against 59% of all coded passages; the windows themselves cover about half the century, so the pull is real but gentle. Legitimation peaks twice, in the 1960s and the 1980s, the decades of Cold War curriculum money and the geographic-illiteracy panic. Licensing a method is the commonest move throughout and dominates the 1930s and again from the 1990s, when the Standards and the spatial-thinking frameworks arrive. Refusing the question peaks in the 1970s. The readings were made by Claude Science (Anthropic) and are themselves interpretations; the sheet carries each passage’s rationale.

The data

Every pin comes from one CSV of passages found by scanning the Journal for sentences that define geography: 1,549 passages with year, author, title, volume, DOI and the surrounding text, including the passages that were read and not pinned. A second, smaller CSV holds the 359 passages that state a definition outright, each with a coded rhetorical function and, since 27 November 2026, a school code; the code reads the passage as a whole, so where one passage holds several definitions its pins may sit on different ridges.
